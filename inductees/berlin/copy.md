# Irving Berlin

**Opening line:**
Around 1893 a family of Russian Jews fleeing persecution landed in New York with almost nothing. Their youngest son, a boy called Israel Baline, would grow up to write "God Bless America."

**Standfirst:**
Irving Berlin was born Israel Baline in the Russian Empire in 1888 and came to America as a child refugee, growing up on New York's Lower East Side. ## A refugee on the Lower East Side

Berlin was born Israel Baline on 11 May 1888 in the Russian Empire. The exact town is genuinely uncertain: the standard reference works give Tyumen, in Siberia, while Berlin's biographers have more often pointed to Tolochin, in what is now Belarus. His father was a cantor. About 1893 the family fled the persecution of Russia's Jews and sailed for the United States, settling in an immigrant tenement district — Manhattan's Lower East Side.

His father died while Israel was still a boy, and he left school to help support the family, selling newspapers and singing for coins. He became a singing waiter in the saloons of the Bowery, and there began putting his own words to the tunes of the day.

## From Baline to Berlin

In 1907 he published his first song, "Marie From Sunny Italy." A printer set his name on the sheet music as "I. Berlin," and he kept it. In 1911 "Alexander's Ragtime Band" made him famous on both sides of the Atlantic. He could barely read music and played the piano mostly in a single key, using a special transposing instrument — and he still became one of the most enduring songwriters his adopted country has produced.

## The oath and the Army

Berlin took his oath as a United States citizen on 6 February 1918, after several years of paperwork. Within months the new citizen was drafted. Stationed at Camp Upton in Yaphank, on Long Island, he wrote an all-soldier Broadway show, *Yip! Yip! Yaphank!*, which raised tens of thousands of dollars before its cast of soldiers shipped out to France. One of its songs, a grumble about the morning bugle, long outlived the war.

It was around this time, too, that he first drafted a patriotic song he judged too solemn, and put it away in a drawer.

## The songs that became America's

In 1938, with war returning to Europe, Berlin took that song back out and reworked it. The singer Kate Smith introduced "God Bless America" on the radio on 11 November 1938, and Berlin assigned its royalties to a fund for the Boy and Girl Scouts. Four years later, in the film *Holiday Inn*, he introduced "White Christmas," which became one of the best-selling songs ever recorded and won him an Academy Award.

So it happened that a Jewish immigrant from the Russian Empire wrote the song many Americans sing at Christmas, and the one many reach for at moments of national feeling — something he seems to have understood as a debt repaid to the country that had taken his family in.

## What the record shows, exactly

A few things about Berlin are worth stating carefully. His birthplace is genuinely unsettled, and a museum should say so rather than choose one town for tidiness. His stage name was not chosen but printed by accident, and kept. And his citizenship has a precise date — 6 February 1918 — that sits strikingly against his reputation: the man who would write "God Bless America" had been, in law, a foreigner for the first twenty-five years he lived in the country. He died in New York in 1989, at the age of 101.

## Discussion questions

1. Berlin arrived as a refugee child and wrote "God Bless America." Why might a newcomer to a country, rather than someone born there, write its most heartfelt patriotic songs?
2. His birthplace is uncertain and his name came from a printer's error. How much does a person's exact origin matter to who they become?
3. Berlin gave away the royalties to "God Bless America." What does it mean to treat a gift to your country as repaying a debt?
4. For twenty-five years Berlin lived in America without being a citizen. What is the difference between belonging to a place and holding its citizenship?
5. Berlin wrote songs for American Christmas though he was Jewish, and for the Army though he arrived as a refugee. How can an outsider come to shape the traditions of the inside?

## Activities

**1. Follow one song.** Pick "God Bless America" or "White Christmas" and find out, from sources you can open, when it was written, who first performed it, and how it was used afterward. Note one thing that surprised you about a song you thought you knew.

**2. Map a journey of uncertainty.** Berlin's birthplace is disputed. Choose an immigrant in your own family or community whose early details are unclear, and list the records — a manifest, a census, a naturalisation file — that might settle them, and where each would be held.
