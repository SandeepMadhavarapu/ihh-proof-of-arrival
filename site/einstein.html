In January 1936, in a New Jersey courthouse, the most famous scientist in the world put his name to a government form declaring that he meant to become an American.

Albert Einstein

Albert Einstein left Germany in 1933 as the Nazis came to power, and never went back. He settled at the Institute for Advanced Study in Princeton, filed his first citizenship papers in 1936, and took the oath as an American in 1940 — one of the few immigrants whose surname became a byword for genius.

The document behind this story

Declaration of Intention for Albert Einstein — U.S. District Court (Petitions for Naturalization), 1936-01-15.

This is Einstein's "first papers." A Declaration of Intention is the statement an immigrant makes under oath that they intend to become a citizen and to renounce allegiance to any foreign state. Einstein filed his in a federal court in New Jersey on 15 January 1936; the original is held by the National Archives, which places it in the public domain. The scan itself, with numbered notes on what each part of the form records, is in this page's Look closer section.

National Archives, Records of District Courts of the United States, RG 21 (National Archives Identifier 596270). Rights, in the institution’s words: “Public Domain, Free of Known Copyright Restrictions (CC Public Domain Mark 1.0).” View the record.

“As long as I have any choice, I will only stay in a country where political liberty, toleration, and equality of all citizens before the law are the rule.” Albert Einstein, public statement on leaving Germany, 10 March 1933 (item 8).

The story

A student in Switzerland, not Germany

Einstein was born in Ulm, in the kingdom of Württemberg, in 1879, and grew up in Munich. But he finished his training across the border: in July 1900 he completed his studies at the Federal Polytechnic — the ETH — in Zurich with a teaching diploma. He became a Swiss citizen soon after, and kept that citizenship for the rest of his life.

In 1905, working as a clerk in the Swiss patent office in Bern, he published the papers that remade physics. He submitted his doctoral dissertation to the University of Zurich that spring; the faculty approved it in July 1905, and the degree was formally conferred on 15 January 1906.

The physicist the whole world knew

Einstein held professorships in Zurich, Prague and Berlin, and in 1921 he was awarded the Nobel Prize in Physics. The citation named his discovery of the law of the photoelectric effect — not, as is often assumed, relativity. By the early 1930s he was the most recognisable scientist alive, and a Jew in a Germany turning openly and violently antisemitic.

1933: the year he did not go home

Einstein was abroad — lecturing in the United States — when Hitler became chancellor in January 1933. He resigned from the Prussian Academy of Sciences, gave up his German citizenship, and resolved not to return. In a public statement that March he set the condition under which he would live anywhere, the sentence quoted above this story.

The Institute for Advanced Study, a new research centre in Princeton, had offered him a post. The Institute opened on 2 October 1933; Einstein, who had still been in Europe, crossed the Atlantic and arrived to take it up later that same month. He would live in Princeton for the rest of his life.

Becoming an American

On 15 January 1936, Einstein filed his Declaration of Intention — an immigrant's sworn "first papers," the formal statement that he meant to become a citizen. The original is held by the National Archives. He took the oath of citizenship in 1940, keeping the Swiss citizenship he had held since young adulthood, so that he ended his life a citizen of two republics and no empire.

He used his fame as an American deliberately: for refugees fleeing the same Europe he had escaped, for civil rights, and against the misuse of the physics he had helped create. He died in Princeton in 1955.

What the record shows, exactly

Einstein's dates are garbled surprisingly often, and a museum should be exact. His Zurich diploma is dated 1900. His Zurich doctorate was conferred in January 1906 — not 1905, the year the dissertation was written and approved; the two are a year apart and both are frequently misreported. He came to the United States to stay in 1933, the year the Institute opened, not in 1932, when he was only a visitor. He was born in Ulm in 1879 and died in Princeton on 18 April 1955.

Questions to talk about

  1. Einstein gave up his German citizenship before any government took it from him. What does it mean to renounce a country, and how is it different from being expelled from one?
  2. He became an American but kept his Swiss citizenship. Can a person belong fully to two countries at once? Should they have to choose?
  3. Einstein was welcomed as a world-famous genius. How might the immigration of a celebrated scientist differ from the immigration of an ordinary refugee fleeing the same danger?
  4. The Nobel committee honoured Einstein for the photoelectric effect, not relativity. Why might even the experts hesitate over the work he is now most famous for?
  5. Einstein used his American fame to speak for refugees and civil rights. What do newcomers owe the country that takes them in, and what does it owe them?

Things to try

  1. Read a real immigration document. Find out what a Declaration of Intention asks an immigrant to state — birthplace, former nationality, date and port of arrival, a physical description, a renunciation of allegiance. Then imagine filling one in for yourself or a relative, and list which questions would be easy to answer and which would be hard.
  2. Separate the fact from the legend. Using only the dates on this page, build a short timeline of Einstein's life from Ulm in 1879 to Princeton in 1955. Mark the two dates most often reported wrongly — his doctorate and his arrival to stay — and note the correct year beside each.

Typed entries as they appear on the form, including the categories the 1930s form required. [n] marks a letter partly hidden by an overprinted label. Handwritten signatures are marked [signature].

TRIPLICATE (To be given to declarant) No. 1442 UNITED STATES OF AMERICA DECLARATION OF INTENTION (Invalid for all purposes seven years after the date hereof)

United States of America District of New Jersey ss: In the District Court of The United States at Trenton, N. J.

I, Dr. Albert Einstein now residing at 112 Mercer St., Princeton Mercer N.J. occupation Professor, aged 56 years, do declare on oath that my personal description is: Sex Male, color White, complexion Fair, color of eyes Brown color of hair Grey, height 5 feet 7 inches; weight 175 pounds; visible distinctive marks none race Hebrew; nationality German

I was born in Ulm Germany on March 14 1879 I am married. The name of my wife [a typed word struck out] is Elsa we were married on April 6th 1917, at Berlin Germany; she or he was born at Hechinge[n] Germany, on January 18, 1877, entered the United States at New York N.Y., on June 3, 1935, for permanent residence therein, and now resides at With me I have 2 children, and the name, date and place of birth, and place of residence of each of said children are as follows: Albert born 5-14-1905 and Eduard born 6-28-1910 both born and reside in Switzerland

I have not heretofore made a declaration of intention: my last foreign residence was Bermuda Great Britain I emigrated to the United States of America from Bermuda Great Britain my lawful entry for permanent residence in the United States was at New York N.Y. under the name of Albert Einstein, on June 3, 1935 on the vessel SS Queen of Bermuda

I will, before being admitted to citizenship, renounce forever all allegiance and fidelity to any foreign prince, potentate, state, or sovereignty, and particularly, by name, to the prince, potentate, state, or sovereignty of which I may be at the time of admission a citizen or subject; I am not an anarchist; I am not a polygamist nor a believer in the practice of polygamy; and it is my intention in good faith to become a citizen of the United States of America and to reside permanently therein; and I certify that the photograph affixed to the duplicate and triplicate hereof is a likeness of me: So help me God.

[signature] Albert Einstein

Subscribed and sworn to before me in the office of the Clerk of said Court, at Trenton, N. J. this 15th day of January anno Domini 1936. Certification No. 3-120742 from the Commissioner of Immigration and Naturalization showing the lawful entry of the declarant for permanent residence on the date stated above, has been received by me.

George T. Cranmer Clerk of the U. S. District Court. By [signature] Deputy Clerk.

[photograph, signed] Albert Einstein (The seal of the court will be impressed so as to cover a portion of the photograph)

Form 2202-L-A U. S. DEPARTMENT OF LABOR IMMIGRATION AND NATURALIZATION SERVICE No. 5773
