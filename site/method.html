Method

How this was made, and the one thing we refused to build

This page exists because a museum should be able to show its working. It records what we did, what we declined to do for the Immigrant Hall of Honor even though the brief invited it, and the evidence behind both decisions.

The refusal

Slide 5 of the IHH challenge brief lists three possible resource types. The third is “AI generated video and audio of or about inductees.” We did not build it, and we are not going to.

Four reasons, in order of weight

1. Slide 3 forbids it

The same brief says, on the previous slide, “All content should be reliable and verifiable.” A generated sentence has no source. That is not a limitation to be engineered around — it is what generation is. You cannot hand a visitor a synthesised Einstein and also hand them the record it came from, because there isn’t one. The two instructions in this brief point in opposite directions, and we took the stricter one.

2. The field has already run this experiment, in public

In July 2023 Khan Academy shipped an AI Harriet Tubman inside its tutoring product. Washington Post testing found the bot failed basic fact checks. Khan Academy’s defence was that the bot “is not intended to function as a historical record of events.”

That sentence saves an ed-tech product. It destroys a museum. Being the historical record is not a feature of a Hall of Honor — it is the whole job.

The public reaction is also on the record: Black users were reported as “horrified at the thought of digitally exhuming a venerated icon and ancestor” in Harriet Tubman. An organisation whose purpose is to honour people would be taking that risk with the exact constituency it exists to serve.

3. Five of the ten inductees died within living memory

Hedy Lamarr (2000), Bob Hope (2003), Celia Cruz (2003), I. M. Pei (2019) and Madeleine Albright (2022). Their families and estates exist and can be asked. Several states now recognise a post-mortem right against digital replicas — New York Civil Rights Law §50-f runs for forty years after death, and Tennessee’s ELVIS Act was enacted in 2024 specifically to cover synthesised voice. Synthesising Madeleine Albright’s voice four years after her death is not a grey area anyone needs to explore on a nonprofit’s behalf.

4. It is what everyone else will build

Across three museum-themed hackathon project galleries we read, the AI historical-figure persona is the single most common entry. It is the obvious idea. Being the team that noticed why it is a bad one is worth more than being the fourth team to demonstrate it.

What we would do instead, if IHH wants it

The refusal is not a dead end. There is a version of slide 5 that works, and it needs three things rather than a model: written consent from the estate or the living subject; a visible label on every synthesised second, not a disclaimer in a footer; and a published policy saying what IHH will and will not synthesise, so the answer is on the record before the first request arrives. We will draft that policy. We will not ship the audio without it.

Slide 5’s second item — “images of artifacts with AI generated annotation” — is a different proposition and a reasonable one, because an annotation can be checked against the object. The rule we applied throughout: a machine may draft, a human verifies, and the page shows the visitor which source settled it. We built that one: Look closer.

What we built instead, and the evidence for it

The mechanic

Cesário & Nisi (2022) ran design workshops with 155 teenagers aged 15–19 in 46 groups. All 46 groups reached for game mechanics — clues and riddles, treasure hunts, quizzes, puzzle collection, perspective-taking. Only 9 of 46 produced strong story elements. Their recommendations: keep it simple rather than feature-rich, avoid time pressure, and reward slow, reflective engagement.

What we did with it

The Verification Desk is challenge, discovery and collection — the three mechanics that study found teenagers reach for — with the timer and the score deliberately removed, because the same study found time pressure makes visitors run through a museum. You can change any verdict forever and the curator’s reasoning appears whether you agreed or not.

The honest caveat

That study is what teenagers said they wanted in a co-design setting, not a measured outcome comparison. Single region, Madeira. Data collected 2017. In-gallery mobile experiences, not web pages. It is the best evidence we found and it is not proof, and we would rather say so than let a citation do work it cannot do.

The number that decided the engineering

Nolte and colleagues (CSCW 2020) tracked 592 projects across 73 hackathons to see what happened after everyone went home.

  • 35.30%had even one commit after the event
  • 17.06%were still active by day six
  • 3.55%were still alive after five months

Winning a prize helped in the short term — the odds ratio for short-term continuation was 6.43. But the finding that should worry every team in this competition is the other one: projects with intense activity in the first week after a hackathon had an increased risk of being discontinued, a 12% rise in the hazard ratio per unit of commit frequency. Furious post-demo activity predicts death, not survival.

So we did not optimise for how much we could build. We optimised for how little has to keep working.

  • No framework, no build step, no package manager. There is nothing to update and nothing whose maintainer can walk away.
  • No API key, no account, no third-party host. Nothing on this site can expire, be rate-limited, start charging, or be switched off by a company we have no relationship with.
  • No database. The Verification Desk holds your findings in the browser tab and forgets them when you close it. There is no personal data to protect because none is collected.
  • 584 KB of HTML, CSS, scripts and text across twenty-two pages (portrait and archival image files are additional), against 2,869 KB for the current IHH homepage alone.
  • A new case file is a list of claims and a list of sources. No code, no design work, no developer. That is the test: not whether it impresses once, but whether the people who own it can still add to it a year from now.

How this maps to what the brief actually asked for

Slide by slide, with the gaps marked rather than glossed.

The sponsor’s stated asks, what we delivered against each, and its status
The brief saysWhat we didStatus
“Choose at least four of our current inductees” All ten. Every current inductee has a full profile, on this site and as a WordPress-ready block in handover/. Five of them also have a case file on the Verification Desk. Exceeded
“Begin with existing IHH website content and increase depth” Every statement on IHH’s ten inductee pages was checked against outside sources while the profiles were researched, and each profile goes beyond it to the records behind the story. The detailed findings go to IHH’s editors privately. Done
“All content should be reliable and verifiable” Every profile lists and links its sources, with each institution’s rights wording; the Verification Desk names forty-seven pieces of evidence, thirty of them linked. No profile has a named person’s editorial sign-off yet. Sourced; human sign-off pending
“Locating complementary multi-media resources” Each profile has an item-level media manifest (institution, stable URL, verbatim rights wording). Interviews and recordings held by other archives are linked from the profiles, and handover/MEDIA-PERMISSIONS.md records who holds each one and how to ask. Located; permissions pending
“Obtain appropriate resources” The images on the profiles were downloaded and viewed, each with its rights wording. Recordings held by other archives stay link-only until their holders give permission; a request letter is drafted, but none has been sent. Partly done
“Ensure accuracy, accessibility, and open access” Every institution’s rights statement reproduced word for word. Zero axe-core violations across every page of this site, best-practice rules included. Every control meets the WCAG 2.2 24×24 target minimum. No screen-reader user has tested it. Tested by machine only
“A format that can be easily integrated into the existing web site” The deliverables are ten WordPress-ready profiles in handover/ — each one Custom HTML block, zero JavaScript, all styling scoped, nothing loaded from anywhere else. This standalone site is the optional extra, not the deliverable. Done
“Engage site visitors, especially young people” The Verification Desk, built on the three mechanics the Cesário & Nisi teenage co-design study found 46 of 46 groups reached for, with the time pressure that study warns against deliberately removed; questions and activities on every profile; and Margin Notes, which let a visitor respond to the exact passage that prompted them (switched off here until IHH connects it to WordPress moderation). Built, not yet tested on readers
“Images of artifacts with AI generated annotation” Look closer: four public-domain or unrestricted records (two patents, a 1935 visa document and a 1936 citizenship declaration) with 25 numbered notes, each showing the exact words it rests on. The notes were drafted with AI; a script refuses to build if any name, number or date in a note is missing from its quoted source. Works without JavaScript, and ships as four static WordPress blocks. No museum editor has reviewed the notes yet. Built; editor review pending
“AI generated video and audio of or about inductees” Declined, with four reasons and an alternative path set out above. Refused

What we have not done

No profile has a named person’s editorial sign-off yet; each says so at the foot of the page. No screen-reader user has tested any of this, and no volunteer has run the WordPress handoff on the museum’s real site. No permission has been requested from any institution and no letter has been sent. The factual corrections in our research should be re-checked by a second person before IHH changes a page. And we still need to confirm with IHH what makes a profile “successful”, which decides the honorarium.
