Madeleine Albright

Madeleine Albright arrived in the United States as a refugee twice over14 — a child whose family fled Hitler, went home, and then fled Stalin. Fifty years after that second arrival, almost to the day, she stood at a naturalization ceremony and took the oath of citizenship again, alongside people taking it for the first time. (The Associated Press reported ninety-seven new citizens from thirty-one countries; an earlier version of this page gave eighty-six, a figure we could not source and have withdrawn.)

Her life, in five parts

Two flights, 1939 and 1948

She was born Marie Jean Korbel in Prague on 15 May 193710 — the US State Department’s Office of the Historian records her birth name as “Marie Jean Madlenka Korbel”; the Czech form Marie Jana Korbelová is in common use, but we did not verify it at any Czech institutional source. Before she was two, her family left. By April 1939, with Prague under German occupation, they had reached London, where her father Josef Korbel joined the Czechoslovak government-in-exile; they stayed through the war.14 Then they went home. In 1948 the communist coup forced them out a second time: the same family, driven from the same country, by opposite regimes, nine years apart. (An earlier version of this paragraph quoted the 1998 State Department statement here. That quotation is withdrawn — we have never opened the document.)

Denver

The family landed in New York in November 1948; she was eleven years old. (The exact day is usually given as 11 November, on the authority of a State Department statement we have not read. The Verification Desk records that claim as not supported for exactly that reason. We give the month.) They did not stay on the coast: they settled in Denver, Colorado, a long way from the ports where most arrivals stopped and from the Czech émigré networks of the eastern cities. She finished her schooling there.

Becoming a citizen, 1957

She became a United States citizen in 1957, nine years after arriving.10 The Department of State’s official biography records the year. It does not record the month, the court, or the certificate number — and no other published source we could find records them either. The document itself has not been digitised and is not described at item level in the National Archives Catalog. What survives in the public record is the year, and nothing more.

Secretary of State, 1997

President Clinton nominated her on 5 December 1996. The Senate confirmed her on 22 January 1997.10 She was sworn in the following day as the first woman to serve as United States Secretary of State, and held the office until 20 January 2001. The commission appointing her was signed by the President and by Acting Secretary of State Peter Tarnoff.

What she learned in 1997

In 1997, as she was becoming Secretary of State, reporting by the Washington Post established what her family had not told her: the family was Jewish, and the three grandparents still living when the war began had died in the concentration camps.11 The total is less settled. Her own later figure, written in her memoir, was more than twenty relatives; a 1997 wire report gave “more than a dozen.”13 Her maternal grandmother was taken to Theresienstadt; what happened after is uncertain — one account traces her onward toward Poland, perhaps to Trawniki, while the same wire report records her fate as simply unknown. She wrote her own account fifteen years afterwards, in Prague Winter: A Personal Story of Remembrance and War, 1937–1948 (2012).

The destination after Theresienstadt is uncertain in the sources, which say “perhaps.” That uncertainty is preserved here rather than resolved.

“Today marks a new beginning in your lives and an ongoing chapter in the story of America which is, above all else, the story of immigrants.”

Madeleine Albright at a naturalisation ceremony, as reported by the Associated Press and carried by CBS News, 10 November 1998. An earlier version of this page printed a longer wording — opening “To all of you who take the oath of citizenship today, I offer my warmest congratulations” — attributed to the State Department statement. We have never read that statement, so the longer wording is withdrawn. The AP report also gives 97 new citizens from 31 countries. Our earlier figure of 86 has no source we can now identify and is withdrawn; where a figure appears on this page it is the wire report’s, attributed to it.

Discussion questions

  • The Korbel family left Czechoslovakia twice, in 1939 and 1948, driven out by opposite political regimes. What does that suggest about why people become refugees?
  • Albright renewed an oath she had already taken forty-one years earlier. Why might someone choose to do that in public?
  • The photograph at the top shows her speaking at a ceremony she once sat through herself. What changes about the picture once you know that?
  • Her citizenship year is recorded, but the document is not. What should a museum do when the fact survives and the paper does not?
  • She learned her family’s history from a newspaper rather than from her family. How should a museum tell a story its subject learned about late?

  4. Photograph from the same series, NAID 505632159 — Bureau of Public Affairs, US Department of State, 1997–2001, RG 59. Second frame from the same file unit; subject not confirmed.
  5. Photograph from the same series, NAID 505632157 — Bureau of Public Affairs, US Department of State, 1997–2001, RG 59. Third frame from the same file unit; subject not confirmed.
  6. Secretary Albright’s Commission — National Museum of American Diplomacy, accession 2012.0022.05. A duplicate of the original commission. The museum describes it as a duplicate; any caption must say so.
  7. Secretary Albright Commemorates 50 Years in the United States — U.S. Department of State, Office of the Spokesman, 10 November 1998. The natural centrepiece of this story, and the one item in this list that nobody working on it has ever opened: the host refuses every request. We cite it; we do not quote it.
  8. Albright Renews Her Oath — Associated Press, carried by CBS News, 10 November 1998. The reachable report of the same ceremony. The source for the ninety-seven new citizens from thirty-one countries, and for the words quoted on this page.
  9. Madeleine Korbel Albright Papers — Library of Congress, Manuscript Division, finding aid ms018034. The finding aid records the gift years as 2014 and 2016; 2015 is the year the Library announced the donation. Not digitised at item level. Her personal archive. On site and by appointment.
  10. Madeleine Korbel Albright — U.S. Department of State, Office of the Historian. Nomination, confirmation, service dates and the 1957 citizenship year. Renders her birth name in anglicised form.
  11. Madeleine Albright didn’t know she was Jewish until The Post told her — The Washington Post, 2022. The Post, whose reporting established that her family was Jewish and that her three surviving grandparents died in the camps.
  12. Prague Winter: a searing account of hidden Jewish family history — j. the Jewish News of Northern California, 2012. A review of her memoir, used as a secondary check on its account.
  13. Behind the Headlines: Albright — Jewish Telegraphic Agency, 15 July 1997. A 1997 wire report; gives “more than a dozen” relatives where her own later figure is more than twenty, and records her grandmother’s fate as unknown.
  14. Prague Winter: A Personal Story of Remembrance and War, 1937–1948 — Madeleine Korbel Albright with Bill Woodward, 2012. Her own account of the family history she learned about in 1997, and of the two flights. The source to cite for anything beyond what is on this page.

Considered and excluded, with reasons

  • UN Photo UN7720438 (Kofi Annan with Albright, 14 January 1997) and UN7935984 (memorial, 17 May 2022). The item records state no terms at all. UN Photo’s separate guidelines require “prior written permission … to reproduce UN photos in print or electronic format, including CDs, DVDs, web sites, videos, exhibits,” and state that UN photos “cannot be altered, sold, redistributed or used to create derivative works.” A museum web page is inside that requirement. Excluded pending a written request.
  • Photographs of her brooches. Strongly associated with her, but the available photographs carry named photographers. Appearing on a government website does not by itself establish unrestricted reuse. Excluded.
  • State Department Flickr. No contemporaneous imagery exists: she left office on 20 January 2001 and Flickr launched in 2004. Use RG 59 series 59-PA-366 instead.
  • Her 1948 arrival manifest and 1957 naturalisation record. Neither is described at item level in the National Archives Catalog; both would require a records request. Not used — and their absence is stated in the text above rather than hidden.
