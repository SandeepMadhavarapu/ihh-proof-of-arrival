On 14 September 2016, sixty-eight years after she arrived in New York as an eleven-year-old refugee, Madeleine Albright stood in the National Archives and welcomed a room of new Americans.

Madeleine Albright

Madeleine Albright arrived in the United States as a refugee twice over — a child whose family fled Hitler, went home, and then fled Stalin. She became the first woman to serve as Secretary of State. She was fifty-nine before she learned what had happened to the family left behind.

Her life, in five parts

Two flights, 1939 and 1948

Marie Jean Korbel was born in Prague on 15 May 1937; the US State Department's Office of the Historian records her birth name as "Marie Jean Madlenka Korbel." The Czech form Marie Jana Korbelová is in common use; we did not verify it at a Czech source, so it appears here as a variant, not a correction. Her father Josef was a Czechoslovak diplomat. In 1939, under German occupation, the family fled to London, where he joined the government-in-exile; she spent the war a small child in a bombarded city. They returned afterwards. In February 1948 the Communist takeover drove them out again, for good. She was eleven, and leaving the same country twice.

Denver

The family settled in Denver, where her father taught at the University of Denver and built its school of international studies. She arrived speaking the language but not the idiom. Decades later, at a naturalisation ceremony, she returned to that gap between sounding American and belonging — recorded here in the one line a wire report carried (source item 8). Her fuller remarks that day survive only in the State Department statement we could not open, so this page does not reproduce them.

Becoming a citizen, 1957

She became a United States citizen in 1957. That year is consistently reported and we can go no further: no month, no court, no certificate number, and no digitised record that we were able to locate. The naturalisation file exists somewhere in the federal system. It is not online, and this page does not pretend otherwise.

Secretary of State, 1997

She had served as United States Permanent Representative to the United Nations from 1993, and was sworn in on 23 January 1997 as the sixty-fourth Secretary of State — the first woman to hold the office. The page states the boundary rather than letting the superlative float: first woman Secretary of State, not first woman in the line of presidential succession. As a naturalised citizen she was ineligible for the presidency, so this was the highest office open to her.

What she learned in 1997

Weeks after taking office she learned, from research done for a Washington Post article, that her family was Jewish and that relatives had died in the Holocaust. She had been raised Catholic and told nothing. The Post established that the three grandparents still living when the war began died in the camps; her own later figure, written in her memoir, was more than twenty relatives, while a 1997 wire report gave "more than a dozen." What happened to her maternal grandmother is not settled: one account has her sent to Theresienstadt in 1942 and then toward Poland, "perhaps Trawniki"; the same wire report records her fate as simply unknown. This page keeps all of it, because the uncertainty is in the record and is not ours to resolve.

Five questions worth arguing about

  1. The page can tell you the year Albright became a citizen but not the month, the court or the certificate number. What is the difference between a fact being *confirmed* and a fact being *documented*, and why would a museum care?
  2. Her family fled twice, from two different regimes, nine years apart. How does a second flight change what "refugee" means for a child who has already been one?
  3. Albright learned about her family's history from a newspaper's research rather than from her parents. What obligations does a journalist have when the research will tell someone something like this about their own family?
  4. The sources disagree about her maternal grandmother: one says she was sent to a destination in Poland, "perhaps Trawniki"; another says her fate is simply unknown. Should a museum pick one, or show both? What does each choice cost?
  5. This page carries a photograph of a naturalisation ceremony in 2016, not of her own arrival in 1948. What work is that substitution doing, and should the page say so more loudly than it does?
